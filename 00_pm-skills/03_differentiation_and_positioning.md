## 0. Start with "why": why "others already do it" does not mean "no opportunity"

Before learning any positioning framework, establish three ways of thinking first; otherwise the framework is just a form to fill in.

**Insight 1: The existence of competitors proves the demand, not that the demand has been met.**
[Fact] April Dunford defines "competitive alternatives" as "what customers would do or use if your product did not exist", and stresses that this includes "hiring an assistant" or "doing nothing" (source: readingraphics' chapter summary of *Obviously Awesome* https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-obviously-awesome/). [Inference] This means "Feishu Minutes already does it" is just one row in the list of alternatives. The real question is: when users use these products, which step is still done by hand, from memory, or through repeated confirmation? That step is your entry point.

**Insight 2: Big companies build "horizontal"; what is left for small teams is "the vertical last mile".**
[Fact] a16z partner Joe Schmidt wrote in his 2026-05-27 article "Avoiding Death on the Yellow Brick Road": "The labs really are coming for a huge swath of the application surface", but the value comes "less from the underlying model's raw capability" and more from "the scaffolding around it that makes the output trustworthy, compliant, and operational inside a specific industry"; "The model is fungible underneath; the system of work is not." (https://a16z.com/avoiding-death-on-the-yellow-brick-road/)
[Fact] Hebbia's "In Defense of Vertical Software" calls this the "last mile": companies look alike from afar, but at the level of the team, the desktop, and the individual, compliance markers, templates, and output standards all differ; "Lines of code are the least interesting part of software." (https://www.hebbia.com/blog/in-defense-of-vertical-software)
[Inference] For BriefPilot, "turning meetings into tasks" is a horizontal capability that models and large vendors have already absorbed. What can be built is the "post-meeting segment of the workflow" for one specific type of team. The more concrete that segment is, and the more industry norms it has, the less likely a general-purpose product is to cover it.

**Insight 3: Differentiation is not "we have it too", but "only we have it, and a certain group cares about it intensely".**
[Fact] Dunford's positioning logic chain is: competitive alternatives → your unique attributes → the provable value those attributes bring → the characteristics of customers who care most about that value → the market category that makes the value stand out most (same readingraphics source as above). [Inference] The key link in the chain is "the customers who care most": the same feature (for example, "traceable citations") is a nice-to-have for a sales team, but may be a hard requirement for a research team that writes papers and reproduces experiments. Differentiation often lies not in the feature itself, but in "who will change their behavior for it".

## 1. April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome*: the five-component positioning method

### 1.1 Framework content
[Fact] Positioning consists of five interrelated components (plus one optional item) (source: readingraphics summary https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-obviously-awesome/; Dunford's official site confirms the book "breaks positioning into five components" and includes "three positioning styles" https://www.aprildunford.com/obviously-awesome):

| Component | Definition | Question for BriefPilot |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Competitive alternatives | What customers would do or use if you did not exist (including "do it by hand" and "do nothing") | After a lab group meeting, how do people actually record action items today? Notion? WeChat groups? Not recording at all? |
| 2. Unique attributes | Features or capabilities you have that the alternatives do not | Server-side whitelist validation of citations? A refusal mechanism? A human confirmation gate? |
| 3. Provable value | The benefit the attributes bring, proven with facts or third-party validation | How much rework does "tasks traceable to the original words" save? Needs experiment E3 |
| 4. Target market characteristics | Who the customers are that care most about this value, and how to identify them | Which kinds of teams pay the highest price for "wrong tasks"? |
| 5. Market category | The category framing that makes your unique value most visible | Is it a "meeting assistant", "lab knowledge management", or something else? |
| +1 Relevant trend (optional) | A trend customers already understand that makes the product feel "right on time" | The demand for "verifiable AI" triggered by AI hallucination |
### 1.2 The Ten-Step Process
[Fact] (same source as above)
1. Understand your "happiest customers": the ones who buy quickly, love using the product, and recommend it.
2. Assemble a cross-functional positioning team.
3. Align on a shared positioning vocabulary and set aside old assumptions.
4. List the **real** competitive alternatives.
5. Identify unique attributes.
6. Translate the attributes into 1–4 "value clusters" tied to customer goals.
7. Define the customers who care most about those value clusters.
8. Find the market frame of reference that makes your strengths stand out most.
9. If appropriate, add a supporting trend.
10. Document and share (for example, with a Positioning Canvas).

### 1.3 Three Market-Entry Stances
[Fact] The summary describes three options in step 8: enter an existing market as a leader; enter an existing market as a niche player; or create a new market (same source). [Inference] BriefPilot sits in the "existing market + no resources" position, so the only realistic option is the second one (niche player). This is consistent with Moore's beachhead logic in Section 3.

### 1.4 Why This Framework Fits BriefPilot
[Inference] It starts from "alternatives" rather than "features", which forces you to first acknowledge that products such as Feishu Minutes exist, and only then look for the attributes they do not cover. This matches exactly how the client will challenge the project.
[Fact] In addition, while promoting *Sales Pitch*, Dunford repeatedly stresses that "No decision" (making no decision / keeping the status quo) is the biggest competitive alternative (source: podcast and Lenny's Newsletter entry descriptions https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/a-step-by-step-guide-to-crafting ; the Lenny page contains only a chapter list, not the full text). [Inference] For users such as research teams, who have "no budget and no IT procurement", the real rival is not Otter but "carrying on with a WeChat group + memory".


## 2. Blue Ocean Strategy: Strategy Canvas and the Four Actions Framework

### 2.1 Framework Content
[Fact] The Four Actions Framework was proposed by Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne. It uses four questions to "reconstruct buyer value elements", breaking the trade-off between differentiation and low cost (source: Blue Ocean official site https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/four-actions-framework/ ; the original questions follow):
- **Eliminate**: Which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated?
- **Reduce**: Which factors should be reduced well below the industry standard?
- **Raise**: Which factors should be raised well above the industry standard?
- **Create**: Which factors that the industry has never offered should be created?

The site stresses that "all four questions must be asked, not just one or two". The accompanying ERRC grid is used to lay out the answers in a table.

[Fact] Strategy canvas: the horizontal axis shows the factors the industry currently competes on, and the vertical axis shows the level of investment/offering each player provides on those factors; the line you draw is called the "value curve" (source: Shortform summary https://www.shortform.com/blog/strategy-canvas-blue-ocean-strategy/ ; note that another page of that summary describes the axes incorrectly; to be verified).
[Fact] Classic case, Yellow Tail wine: it eliminated/reduced factors such as complexity, aging, jargon, and prestige, producing a value curve different from both cheap wines and premium wines (source: Process Excellence Network case study https://www.processexcellencenetwork.com/innovation/articles/creating-value-on-the-vine-a-yellow-tail-case-stud/ ).

### 2.2 Steps
1. List the factors everyone in the current "AI meeting assistant" category is competing on (transcription accuracy, multilingual support, live captions, bot joining meetings, CRM integration, sentiment analysis, and so on).
2. Draw the value curves of 2–3 major competitors.
3. Ask the four ERRC questions for each factor.
4. Draw your new curve and check whether it has a different shape, rather than simply being higher overall.

### 2.3 Example for BriefPilot (all [Assumption]; to be validated by user research)
| Action | Factor | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Eliminate | Bot joining meetings, live captions | Research group meetings are often in-person or hybrid, and participants are sensitive about being recorded |
| Reduce | Presentation of the full transcript, sentiment/engagement analysis | Users want conclusions and tasks, not a verbatim transcript |
| Raise | Per-task citation back to the original wording, explicitly leaving missing fields blank, refusal to answer | For people who need reproducibility and write papers, "provenance" is a hard requirement |
| Create | Links from tasks to literature, lab records, and datasets; "which meeting + which paper this decision is based on" | Existing meeting products do not understand research objects |

[Fact] Granola's own positioning is in fact an ERRC exercise: it "eliminated" the meeting bot and recording storage ("no meeting bot", "no recordings stored") and "raised" the blending of the user's handwritten notes with AI (source: Granola official llms.txt https://granola.ai/llms.txt ). [Inference] This shows that, even within the same red-ocean category, elimination alone can produce a perceptible difference.

## 3. Geoffrey Moore, *Crossing the Chasm*: The Beachhead and the Bowling Pins

### 3.1 Framework content
[Fact] In his Lenny's Newsletter interview, Moore gives the criteria for choosing a beachhead segment: "big enough to matter, small enough to lead, and a good fit with your crown jewels". He also stresses locking onto one "critical pain point", then winning a reference customer and using their success story as a reference (source: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/geoffrey-moore-on-finding-your-beachhead ; the page only has a summary of key points, not the full text).
[Fact] (Secondary summary, to be verified) The "whole product" has four layers: generic product, expected product, augmented product, and potential product. Pragmatist customers want a complete solution, not the technology itself. The "bowling pin" strategy means that after taking the beachhead, you move into adjacent segments one by one; each new segment reuses most of the existing whole product and only adds incremental customization (source: multiple book summaries, e.g. https://scottburleson.substack.com/p/book-summary-crossing-the-chasm-geoffrey , https://grahammann.net/book-notes/crossing-the-chasm-geoffrey-moore ).

### 3.2 Why "segment first"
[Inference] For a new product without resources, entering horizontally means competing with every rival on every factor at once. Entering by segment narrows the competition to "the two or three factors this group cares about most", and these are exactly the places where you can "raise/create" using ERRC. Segmenting does not mean giving up the large market; it means advancing through adjacent segments (bowling pins), one pin at a time.

### 3.3 Moore's positioning statement template (the original six lines)
[Fact] Source: productbitesforlife, quoting the third edition of *Crossing the Chasm*: https://productbitesforlife.substack.com/p/clarity-and-discovery-through-value
```
For        (target customer: write only the beachhead segment)
Who        are dissatisfied with (current market alternative)
Our product is a (product category)
That       provides (compelling reason to buy)
Unlike     (product alternative)
We have assembled (key whole-product features for this specific application)
```
[Fact] A common criticism: on Sharebird, a practitioner calls the template "reductive" and says nobody uses it once it is written. They cite Dunford's view that this format lacks staying power and recommend the approach from *Obviously Awesome* instead (https://sharebird.com/h/product-marketing/q/hiim-looking-to-build-a-b2b-positioning-framework-and-a-matrix-for-sign-off-from-various-teams-internally-does-anyone-have-a-template-already-in-place-that-has-worked-well-for-you ). [Inference] A practical approach: use Dunford's five components for the analysis and Moore's template for the one-sentence output; the two do not conflict.
[Fact] Advice shared by several sources on the "Unlike" line: it must name the alternatives buyers are actually considering, not empty phrases like "outdated legacy systems" (https://medium.com/h7w/product-positioning-edfe6679f6f4 ).

### 3.4 BriefPilot example ([Assumption] version, for discussion rather than as a conclusion)
```
For   university research groups (5–20 people) running experiments and writing papers
Who   are dissatisfied with recording group meeting conclusions in WeChat groups + personal notes, and afterwards cannot say clearly "who agreed to do what, and based on which paper"
Our product is a group meeting action-item assistant with an evidence chain
That  provides for every task a link back to the original quote and the relevant literature, leaves missing information explicitly blank, and counts an item only after a human confirms it
Unlike general-purpose transcription tools for enterprise meetings, such as Feishu Minutes / Otter
We have assembled citation verification, refusal to answer, a human confirmation gate, and links to literature / experiment records
```

## 4. Hamilton Helmer, *7 Powers*: Can the Differentiation Be Defended?

### 4.1 Framework content
[Fact] Helmer defines "Power" as "the set of conditions creating the potential for persistent differential returns", which requires both a **Benefit** and a **Barrier** (source: book summary on blas.com, https://blas.com/7-powers/ ). The seven Powers:

| Power | Definition | Where the barrier comes from | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scale economies | The larger the scale, the lower the unit cost | Taking share is costly for a challenger | — |
| Network economies | The more users there are, the more valuable the product is to each user | Winner takes all | — |
| Counter-positioning | A newcomer adopts a superior business model that the incumbent **does not copy** because doing so would damage its existing business | The barrier lies within the rival itself | Vanguard index funds vs Fidelity |
| Switching costs | Customers pay a price to change suppliers | A challenger must compensate customers | — |
| Branding | Reduced uncertainty / emotional value commands a price premium | Requires long-term accumulation (lag) | Brand-name drugs vs generics |
| Cornered resource | Exclusive access to a scarce asset on preferential terms | Legal / exclusive | Pixar's Brain Trust |
| Process power | An operational advantage embedded in the organization | Complex and opaque, hard to replicate | Toyota's TPS |

[Fact] Additional notes on counter-positioning: Helmer holds that the incumbent's failure to follow is a "rational response" rather than stupidity. Incumbent reactions often go through "denial, ridicule, fear, anger, capitulation (often too late)". This Power is not exclusive, and it weakens over time (In-N-Out has it against McDonald's, but not against Five Guys) (sources: https://lukethomas.com/counter-positioning/ , https://www.bprigent.com/article/counter-positioning-7-powers-benefits-barriers-examples-business-strategy , both secondary, to be verified).
### 4.2 How to use it
[Inference] After finishing the differentiation analysis, ask: "Which of the seven Powers does this differentiation belong to? If it is none of them, a competitor can copy it in a few weeks."
Diagnosis for BriefPilot ([Inference]):
- Citation verification, refusal to answer, and confirmation gates: these are all features, not Powers; large vendors can add them.
- Possible counter-positioning: the business logic of large vendors' meeting products is "the more automation, the better" (auto-create tasks, auto-sync to CRM). If BriefPilot insists on "do less rather than guess," large vendors may be unwilling, at least in the short term, to make "conservative" their own selling point. This is weak counter-positioning, [Assumption] to be validated.
- Possible switching costs / process power: if the product accumulates a "decision–meeting–literature" association graph for a lab over many years, switching tools means losing that graph. This is what Hebbia and a16z mean by "workflow as moat" (see the sources in Section 0).
- Conclusion: in v2, BriefPilot needs to deliberately design in the seed of at least one Power; otherwise it is only a demo.

## 5. Peter Thiel, *Zero to One*: Monopoly from a Small Market

[Fact] Thiel's original text (quoted consistently across multiple book excerpts): "Every startup is small at the start. Every monopoly dominates a large share of its market. Therefore, every startup should start with a very small market. Always err on the side of starting too small." (Source: Goodreads quotes https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/10694686-every-startup-is-small-at-the-start-every-monopoly-dominates ; check against the original book before citing)
[Fact] He treats "we only need 1% of a $100 billion market" as a warning sign (Source: https://paulmillr.com/posts/zero-to-one/ , secondary).
[Fact] The PayPal case: it initially could not find users, then turned to a small group, several thousand eBay PowerSellers, and monopolized it first; PayPal was sold to eBay in 2002 for $1.5 billion (Source: Inc. https://www.inc.com/zoe-henry/peter-thiel-how-to-build-a-monopoly.html ; sources disagree on the exact number of PowerSellers, to be verified).
[Fact] Another frequently cited case: Amazon started by selling books (multiple book excerpts).

[Inference] Thiel's "small market" and Moore's "beachhead" are two ways of saying the same thing. The difference is that Thiel puts more emphasis on becoming irreplaceable (a monopoly) in that small market, rather than merely "having share." Implication for BriefPilot: if research labs are chosen, the goal is not "labs can use it too" but "labs have no alternative to it." This requires the product to understand objects unique to labs (papers, experiments, datasets, reproduction, submission deadlines). [Assumption] Whether these objects are a genuine must-have needs to be tested through interviews.

## 6. Wedge Strategy

### 6.1 Definition and criteria
[Fact] NFX defines a "killer wedge" as "a product feature or a small target market that lets you quickly learn from a large number of customers and refine the product," and gives criteria for a good wedge: the feature is paired with a clear customer group; feedback is fast and iteration cycles are short; customers are closely connected, which helps word of mouth spread; decision-makers are reachable; value is immediately measurable; the target group is underestimated, willing to pay, or high-status. NFX also warns: "A wedge is not PMF, nor is it a complete GTM strategy," and "Learn everything you can, but don't stay there forever." (Source: https://nfx.com/post/finding-your-killer-wedge )
[Fact] Every.to's "Product Wedges" defines a wedge as the part of an early product "used to establish new customer relationships," with three selection criteria: easy for customers to adopt (low learning cost, familiar, low risk, embedded in existing workflows); deliverable by the company early on (does not depend on network effects, data, or funding); and clearly connected to the larger vision ("fuzzy paths are risky") (Source: https://every.to/divinations/product-wedges-a-complete-guide ).
[Fact] (To be verified) The term "thin edge of the wedge" is often attributed to Chris Dixon's 2010 article; opening the original directly returned a 404 this time, and only secondary citations are available (https://echai.ventures/glossary/wedge ).
[Fact] There are two common expansion directions: more use cases for the same group (land & expand), or the same use case extended to more groups (Source: https://ntkris.substack.com/p/startups-can-always-win-incumbents , secondary).
[Fact] The view of one seed-stage guide: "the failure mode is not being too narrow, but choosing a wedge with no adjacent market" (https://hub.causo.ai/guides/wedge-strategy-seed ).

### 6.2 The difference between a wedge and a beachhead
[Inference] A beachhead (Moore) is the "who" (a customer segment); a wedge (NFX/Every) can be the "who," or it can be "which feature / which step of the workflow." For BriefPilot, the wedge is more likely a workflow node, for example "within 10 minutes after a group meeting ends, turn spoken commitments into action items with sources," rather than the entire "knowledge Q&A + action items" two-module package.

## 7. Niche-down / Vertical SaaS: Why Vertical Beats Horizontal

### 7.1 Argument and evidence
[Fact] Joe Schmidt of a16z (2026-05) proposes three tests: the "tools-and-steps test" (how many steps and tools the work requires), the "system test" (whether you are the system the customer runs the work on, or a tool layered on top of someone else's system), and the "P&L test" (whether the customer pays for the outcome of a workflow or for general-purpose capability). He argues that model vendors will dominate horizontal single-step tasks (code, writing, images), while multi-step, multi-role vertical workflows with legacy systems and determinism requirements are where startups have room (https://a16z.com/avoiding-death-on-the-yellow-brick-road/ ).
[Fact] Hebbia: legal AI has not become thinner as models have improved; instead "orchestration" (when to trust the model, what data to feed it, how to format the output) matters more. In financial settings, "output that looks right but is slightly wrong is as good as useless" (https://www.hebbia.com/blog/in-defense-of-vertical-software ).
[Fact] Greylock's view: initial data access is only a preliminary moat, while the data generated as customers use the product is the long-term moat (https://greylock.com/blog/vertical-ai/ ; taken from a search snippet, original wording still to be verified).
[Fact] Counterargument: Vendep argues that the "data moat" is a fatal strategic mistake in the AI era, and that the real fortress is the workflow (https://www.vendep.com/post/forget-the-data-moat-the-workflow-is-your-fortress-in-vertical-saas ).
[Fact] Definitions and trade-offs of vertical vs. horizontal: vertical SaaS serves a single industry and goes deep first; horizontal SaaS serves multiple industries with one product and goes broad first; horizontal tends to prevail in very large M&A deals, vertical in mid-sized ones (source: https://ibinterviewquestions.com/guides/tmt-investment-banking/vertical-saas-vs-horizontal-saas and similar pages, all blogs, to be verified).

### 7.2 Why this matters for BriefPilot
[Inference] Meeting minutes are a classic horizontal single-step task (recording → transcript → summary), and the more capable models become, the more commoditized it gets. For BriefPilot to survive, it must move from being a "meeting tool" to being the "working system" of a specific vertical scenario: the minutes are only the entry point, and the downstream "action item – evidence – literature – experiment – retrospective" loop is the actual product.
[Fact] A reminder from Aman Gour, a guest in the a16z article: "The workflow you ship on day one is not the moat." (same source) [Inference] So at the demo stage, do not claim a moat. Claim only that "we chose a workflow the big players will not prioritize, and we can show that users here care more about sources."

## 8. Four Types of Differentiation, with Cases

[Fact] Atlassian's Chinese-language guide defines product differentiation as "highlighting specific attributes, advantages, or features that competitors lack," and divides it into horizontal, vertical, and hybrid types (https://www.atlassian.com/zh/agile/product-management/product-differentiation ). Below, cases are organized using a four-way split that is closer to day-to-day PM practice.

### 8.1 Feature differentiation (doing what competitors don't)
**Case: Granola vs Otter / Fireflies**
- [Fact] Granola describes itself as an "AI notepad for meetings": the user jots down key points during the meeting, and the AI fills in context from the transcript afterward. It sends no bot into the meeting and stores no recordings (audio is captured directly from the microphone and system, then deleted after transcription). Pricing is Free / Business $14 / Enterprise $35+ per user per month. It explicitly lists who it is not for: legal teams that need audio playback, teams that need engagement/sentiment analysis, and Android users (source: https://granola.ai/llms.txt , first-party).
- [Fact] Third-party comparison pages generally say Otter is stronger in real-time transcription and audio playback, and that Granola has no real-time transcription (source: https://circleback.ai/compare/granola-vs-otter-ai and similar pages, all competitor or affiliate pages, to be verified).
- [Inference] Granola's differentiation is "elimination + blending": it removes the bot and the recording, gaining privacy and unobtrusiveness, and it fuses human-written notes with AI. It is not a "better Otter" but a "differently shaped value curve." It also deliberately gives up some customers (legal, analytics-oriented teams), which is exactly the price of positioning.
- [Fact] Granola's page claims that more than half of its users are in management, and its target audience includes founders/executives, VCs, recruiters, PMs, and sales (same source). [Inference] What these people have in common is that "meeting content is sensitive and they don't want the other side to see a bot," so "user differentiation" and "feature differentiation" are bound together.

### 8.2 User differentiation (serving an underestimated group)
**Case: Superhuman vs Gmail**
- [Fact] From the investment note by a16z partner David Ulevitch, 2019-06-27: "the fastest email experience ever made"; "what Gmail would be if it were built today, not 15 years ago"; "everything happens in 100ms or less"; users "get through their inbox twice as fast"; pricing at the time was $30/month; the GTM used "live concierge onboardings"; the target was "tens of millions of power users" (https://a16z.com/2019/06/27/superhuman/ ).
- [Fact] Contrary Research: Starter is $30/user/month, Business $40, Enterprise is custom; onboarding is a 30-minute coaching session; early on, applicants whose needs did not fit were turned away; the page notes "Acquired by Grammarly in 2025" (no deal terms) (https://research.contrary.com/company/superhuman ).
- [Fact] As early as 2017, TechCrunch questioned whether it could get power users to give up free Gmail and pay (https://techcrunch.com/2017/08/18/rapportive-founders-new-startup-superhuman-is-what-gmail-would-be-if-built-today/ ).
- [Inference] Superhuman's differentiation is essentially user differentiation: it does not build "everyone's inbox," only the inbox of "people who spend 3 hours a day in email," and then serves them with features such as speed, a keyboard-driven flow, and hands-on onboarding. Pricing itself also works as a filter.
### 8.3 Experience Differentiation (Same Features, a Different "Feel")
**Case: Linear vs Jira**
- [Fact] The Linear Method page lists eight principles: Build for the creators ("Keeping individuals productive is more important than generating perfect reports"), Purpose-built (unlimited flexibility lets everyone invent their own process, which turns into chaos as the team grows), Create momentum, don't sprint, Meaningful direction, Aim for clarity ("Projects should be called projects"), Say no to busy work, Simple first, then powerful, Decide and move on (https://linear.app/method/introduction ).
- [Fact] Third-party comparisons: Linear has no custom workflow states in the Jira sense, no equally deep custom fields, and no plugin marketplace; Jira wins on reporting and ecosystem; Linear's Jira importer does not preserve custom states, fields, or automation rules (source: https://ones.com/blog/linear-vs-jira-7-key-differences-to-pick-the-right-tool-in-2026/ and others; some are competitor blogs, to be verified).
- [Inference] Linear's differentiation is an "experience built by subtraction": what users want is not more configuration, but a tool that doesn't wear them out when they open it. This is a textbook case of "eliminate/reduce" in the ERRC framework, and it also shows that "less is differentiation" takes courage, because it directly shuts out large enterprises that need complex processes.

### 8.4 Business Model / Category Differentiation (Defining Yourself by Switching Categories)
**Case: Notion vs Evernote**
- [Fact] In an interview with Lenny's Newsletter, Ivan Zhao said Notion's vision has always been to "give anyone the ability to customize software to their needs"; the first version failed because it was "too complex, what I wanted rather than what users needed"; after the restart, the company moved to "notes and docs with a twist", an entry point everyone already understood, and hid the larger vision inside it (his phrase: "sugar-coating the broccoli"); a horizontal product has to balance "Lego bricks" (basic building blocks) against "Lego boxes" (packaged solutions) (https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/inside-notion-ivan-zhao ).
- [Fact] Sequoia records that three years after founding, they threw out and rewrote the code, and Zhao said "It didn't work because the product was not understandable for most people" (https://www.sequoiacap.com/?p=11634 , from a search snippet).
- [Fact] Notion was founded in 2013; it reached 1 million users in 2019 and later grew to more than 30 million (source: https://productmint.com/how-does-notion-make-money/ and other blogs, to be verified). The reasons for Evernote's decline (price increases, moving free features behind a paywall) rest only on blogs and HN comments and are anecdotal (to be verified).
- [Inference] Notion's differentiation is not "better note-taking" but a change of category: from a "note-taking app" to a "workspace you assemble yourself". Once the category changes, Evernote goes from being a rival to being the "old category that got replaced". This corresponds to Dunford's step 8, "find a market frame of reference that makes your strengths stand out". The cost also has to be acknowledged: Notion has a steeper learning curve, and its "freedom" can overwhelm newcomers (mentioned consistently across multiple comparison articles).

### 8.5 Summary of the Four Types and Their Mapping to BriefPilot
| Type | Core question | Case | BriefPilot candidate ([Assumption]) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feature | What do we do that rivals don't | Granola's no-bot approach | Task–citation–literature triad; refusing to answer |
| User | Who do we serve exclusively | Superhuman's power users | Research groups / teams that need reproducibility and paper submissions |
| Experience | How much "smoother" the same thing feels | Linear's subtraction | Confirmation finished within 10 minutes after the meeting, without reading the transcript |
| Business model / category | Which category we place ourselves in | Notion's "workspace" | Not "meeting assistant", but "lab decision record"? |
[Inference] In practice, differentiation usually holds only when two or more of the four types appear together (Granola: feature + user; Superhuman: user + experience + pricing). Feature differentiation alone is the most fragile.

## 9. Competitive Analysis Methods in the Chinese Context

### 9.1 Process: Goal → Select Competitors → Define Dimensions → Collect → Analyze → Report
[Fact] The six-step process common in China comes from Zhang Zaiwang's *Effective Competitive Analysis*: first set the goal, then select competitors, define the analysis dimensions, collect information, organize and analyze it, and produce a report; the report emphasizes "conclusion first" (source: Everyone Is a Product Manager https://www.woshipm.com/share/6109656.html ).

### 9.2 User–Scenario Grid (A Tool for Selecting Competitors)
[Fact] Using "user" and "scenario" as the two axes, competitors are divided into four cells: A core competitors (both users and scenarios overlap heavily), B similar-category competitors (scenarios overlap, users differ), C look-alike competitors (same users, different scenarios), D reference competitors (low overlap, used only as design references). Category A should cover all product forms and be tracked closely, B/C monitored periodically, and D watched occasionally. The author also criticizes the "market, then users, then the five elements of user experience" template as "all-encompassing but only skimming the surface", and advises newcomers to first focus on one functional module and dig deep (https://www.woshipm.com/evaluating/5451468.html ).
[Inference] For BriefPilot: if the target users are set as research groups, Feishu Minutes/Otter may drop from category A to category B (scenarios overlap, users differ), while Notion/Obsidian/Zotero/lab wikis may move up to category A or C. This reclassification is itself part of the differentiation narrative.
### 9.3 $APPEALS (eight dimensions from the user's perspective)
[Fact] $APPEALS is often associated with the IBM/Huawei IPD framework. Its eight elements are: Price, Availability, Packaging, Performance, Ease of use, Assurances, Life cycle cost, and Social acceptance. Usage: set a weight for each element through user surveys, score your own product and competitors on each item, draw a radar chart, then use the weights to find gaps and shape strategy. Internet products often overlook "Price" and can add a "popularity" dimension (https://www.woshipm.com/pmd/3542523.html ). Methods commonly used for Huawei's requirements classification include $APPEALS and BSA/Kano (https://www.woshipm.com/it/4384157.html , secondary source).
[Inference] $APPEALS suits item-by-item benchmarking within the "same category, same users"; it is not suited to discovering opportunities that "change the category". It is more like an industrial version of the Blue Ocean strategy canvas. Use it to confirm "on which dimensions we cannot win", then use ERRC to decide "which dimensions we simply won't compete on".

### 9.4 The four elements: user, scenario, problem, solution
[Fact] This search did not find a formal framework named "user-scenario-problem-solution" (source to be verified). Related material that could be found: woshipm's "user needs are the product of users combined with scenarios"; sspai's understanding of "pain points" as "why users use this product and where its irreplaceable value lies" (https://sspai.com/post/64891 ); and the view that the core of solution analysis is "whether it can solve the original problem, how well it solves it, differentiation and core competitiveness" (https://www.woshipm.com/pmd/4202587.html ).
[Inference] These four elements can be seen as a "simplified Chinese version" of Dunford's five elements: user ≈ target market characteristics, scenario ≈ trigger situation (JTBD), problem ≈ what alternatives cannot do, solution ≈ unique attributes + value. We recommend that BriefPilot's competitor analysis table write every row in five columns: "user / scenario / problem / how existing solutions handle it / how we handle it".

### 9.5 SWOT
[Fact] SWOT analyzes strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats by pulling together internal and external conditions; sources generally consider it "relatively high-level and subjective" (https://www.ai-indeed.com/encyclopedia/16585.html ). [Inference] In interviews, SWOT can be used to close, but not to open; it does not produce insight, it only organizes insight.

## 10. "Others already do it, so why are you still building it?": an answer framework

### 10.1 Why this question deserves a serious answer
[Fact] The consensus in VC circles on "what if Google builds it?": it is a legitimate question, used to test your advantages and your path to winning; don't be defensive, treat it as an opportunity to explain your uniqueness and team fit (https://theventurecrew.substack.com/p/what-if-googleamazon-builds-it-annoying ). Paul Graham's "Why There Aren't More Googles", from an investor's perspective, points out that what is scarce is capital willing to back unproven things (https://paulgraham.com/googles.html ).
[Inference] When an interviewer asks this question, what they are really testing is: whether you have done an analysis of alternatives, whether you have user evidence, whether you have thought about moats, and whether you honestly acknowledge the costs. Being able to answer all four is ten times better than answering "our features are better".

### 10.2 Five-step talking structure (synthesizing Dunford's alternatives logic, Moore's beachhead, Helmer's moats, and a16z's vertical thesis)
[Inference] The structure below is a synthesis by this handbook, not the original text of any single book.

1. **Acknowledge and precisely describe the alternatives (30 seconds)**
   "Yes, Feishu Minutes, Otter, Fireflies, and Granola all do meeting → minutes → tasks. I've classified them along two axes: they all target enterprise meetings, are transcription-centric, and default to the more automation the better."
   Purpose: prove you did your homework and are not unaware of them.
2. **Point out the step they all fail to cover (30 seconds)**
   "But they all stop at ___ (a specific workflow node): once a task is generated, it loses its connection to the original words and to the materials from before the meeting; when information is missing, they guess an owner; and there is no human confirmation gate."
   Purpose: shift the competition from "who transcribes more accurately" to "who is accountable for wrong tasks".
3. **Explain why a particular group of people cares especially much (60 seconds, user evidence required)**
   "For ___ (a specific user segment), the cost of this step is ___ (for example: a wrongly recorded experimental condition means rerunning for two weeks). I interviewed N people, and M of them mentioned ___."
   Purpose: this is the evidence core of the whole talking script; if you have no interview data, honestly say "this is my assumption, and here is how I plan to validate it".
4. **Explain why big companies won't do it first (30 seconds)**
   "This segment is small and not on the big companies' horizontal roadmap (citing a16z: model vendors dominate horizontal single-step tasks); and it requires 'do less, don't guess, leave blanks', which is the opposite of the big companies' selling point of 'the more automation the better' (weak counter-positioning)."
   Purpose: answer the follow-up "they can just add it casually".
5. **Explain the seed of a moat and what you gave up (30 seconds)**
   "We don't do real-time transcription, we don't do sentiment analysis, and we don't send a bot into the meeting; what we accumulate is this team's 'decision–meeting–evidence' association graph, which gets harder to switch away from the longer it is used. There is no moat yet; all I can show is that users care more about provenance here."
   Purpose: draw an honest boundary and show Power awareness.

### 10.3 One-sentence version (Moore template)
See Section 3.4. Practice until you can say it within 20 seconds.

### 10.4 Common follow-up questions and how to respond
| Follow-up | Key points of the response |
|---|---|
| Couldn't Feishu just add a "cite" button? | Acknowledge that it could; the difference is that "citation" is a nice-to-have for enterprise meetings but an entry point for research/compliance scenarios; and big companies adding a feature will not change the product philosophy of "automate by default". |
| Is the market too small? | Cite Thiel: "Always err on the side of starting too small"; explain the bowling pins: labs → R&D teams of research-oriented enterprises → any team that needs auditing. |
| Do you have data? | If not, say you don't; present the experiment design (E1–E4) and the current hypotheses H1–H3. |
| Why you? | You are a lab member yourself, so you can dogfood, and you understand the objects involved (papers, experiments, submissions) rather than only "meetings". |

## 11. Tying the Frameworks Together: BriefPilot v2 Positioning Workflow (Suggested Order)

[Inference] Suggested order of operations, with one file produced per step:
